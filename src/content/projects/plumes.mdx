## Problem

A Philippine eruption's record is scattered: PHIVOLCS publishes daily bulletins,
satellites measure gas and heat, the US Geological Survey lists earthquakes and
the Smithsonian logs eruptions, each in its own archive. Nothing puts them side
by side, so seeing what every instrument recorded around Taal in January 2020
means stitching it together by hand. Plumes is a record of the past, not a
warning service; PHIVOLCS is the sole authority for alert levels.

## Approach

- **Ingest:** 24 public sources, read in place where possible (Google Earth
  Engine, NASA's APIs and cloud-hosted data) rather than downloaded in bulk.
  Crawlers fetch the PHIVOLCS bulletins and Tokyo's volcanic ash advisories.
- **Normalise:** each volcano gets series for SO₂, heat, earthquakes and people
  living nearby. Every series is labelled by instrument and shows how much
  of the area the satellite could see, because ash and cloud hide the
  satellites at the worst moments.
- **Serve:** a map of the 24 active volcanoes, a page per volcano with every
  record on one time axis, and <LiveStoryCount url={frontmatter.dataVersionUrl} label="guided stories" /> in English and Japanese.
- **Integrity:** every chart is dated. PHIVOLCS alert levels appear only as
  dated markers "as reported by PHIVOLCS", each linked to its bulletin, and
  nothing describes a volcano today. An empty series says why on its page.

## Architecture

Standard pattern: Jamstack delivery of materialised read models (the read side
of CQRS), with the pipeline as a batch layer, plus a read-only Spring Boot
service in the hexagonal (ports and adapters) style. Customisations: Earth
Engine does the satellite reductions where the imagery is stored, instead of
downloading it. Each data version is
validated, then published to GitHub Pages, with a Hugging Face mirror as the
fallback. GraalVM compiles the Java 17 API to a native image, which sits behind
an edge cache and wakes fast on a free server. Every source follows the
workspace archiving standard: a registry, fallbacks and a private archive.

## Where it stands

Plumes is live at plumes.krtmtthw.dev. The overview map filters satellite heat
detections and earthquakes by month, with layers for monthly mean SO₂ from
Sentinel-5P and ground motion along the satellite's line of sight (COMET
LiCSBAS). Each volcano's time axis runs from 2000 and carries a deep-time strip of its
recorded eruptions. The site also has <LiveStoryCount url={frontmatter.dataVersionUrl} label="guided stories" /> and an About page listing every
source; the code is [public on GitHub](https://github.com/abrycta/plumes).

Each data version goes live only when every check passes: each file matches its
schema, each day has one bulletin row linked to its source, and known facts hold.
The records draw on 5,290 bulletin pages and 6,084 archive bulletins from
PHIVOLCS, 2,105 Tokyo ash advisories since 2010 and 649,248 NASA FIRMS heat
detections.

The Taal story is told from the data. PHIVOLCS reported Alert Level 1 every day
from 1 December 2019 to 11 January 2020, then raised it to level 4 on 12
January. The USGS catalogue had no earthquake within 30 km from October 2019
until 12 January, then 35 that day. Tokyo's ash centre observed ash up to about
16.8 km, and Sentinel-2 shows the crater lake drained.

PHIVOLCS's online bulletins start in September 2017 for Mayon, Kanlaon and
Bulusan and in March 2019 for Taal; from July 2022 they are in its LAVA archive.

## Problem

The record of a Philippine eruption is scattered. PHIVOLCS publishes daily
bulletins, satellites measure the gas and heat, the US Geological Survey lists
the earthquakes, and the Smithsonian logs the eruptions, each in its own archive
and format. Nothing puts them side by side, so seeing what every instrument
recorded around Taal in January 2020 means stitching it together by hand.
Plumes is a record of the past, not a warning service: PHIVOLCS is the sole
authority for alert levels.

## Approach

- **Ingest:** 24 sources, read in place wherever possible (Google Earth Engine,
  NASA's APIs and cloud-hosted data) instead of bulk downloads, plus polite
  crawlers for PHIVOLCS bulletins and Tokyo's volcanic ash advisories.
- **Normalise:** a daily series per volcano for SO₂, heat, earthquakes and
  people living nearby, each labelled by instrument and shown with how much of
  the area the satellite could actually see.
- **Serve:** a map of 24 active volcanoes, a page per volcano with every record
  on one time axis, and guided stories starting with Taal in January 2020, in
  English and Japanese.
- **Integrity:** every chart is dated, PHIVOLCS values appear only as linked,
  dated markers, and nothing describes a volcano today.

## Architecture

Chosen in planning: static read models built by a Python pipeline in GitHub
Actions, with Earth Engine doing the satellite reductions, served from a CDN;
and a read-only Java 17 Spring Boot API on PostGIS, compiled to a native image
with GraalVM so it wakes fast on a free server, behind an edge cache. Every
source follows the workspace archiving standard: a registry, fallbacks and a
private archive.

## Where it stands

Planning done on 2026-10-21; a measurement spike on 2026-10-22 confirmed the
plan. Taal's January 2020 eruption shows clearly: SO₂ within 15 km rose to
about 80 times its normal level on 13 January, satellites detected heat on the
volcano from 13 to 26 January, and the earthquake count went from none to 35 on
12 January. NASA's eruption database lists 18 Philippine eruptions with their
SO₂ mass and plume height, and Taal's full 2000–2026 data file is 49 kB
compressed. Milestone 1 is in progress: the data pipeline, the map and the Taal
story.

Known limitations to design for: ash and cloud hide the satellites at the worst
moments, so coverage is shown beside every value; PHIVOLCS's newer bulletins
(from July 2022) are only on its LAVA system; and records before weather
satellites are sparser.

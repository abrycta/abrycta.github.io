## Problem

The record of a Philippine eruption is scattered. PHIVOLCS publishes daily
bulletins, satellites measure the gas and heat, the US Geological Survey lists
the earthquakes, and the Smithsonian logs the eruptions, each in its own archive
and format. Nothing puts them side by side, so seeing what every instrument
recorded around Taal in January 2020 means stitching it together by hand.
Plumes is a record of the past, not a warning service: PHIVOLCS is the sole
authority for alert levels.

## Approach

- **Ingest:** 24 public sources chosen. Wherever
  possible they are read in place (Google Earth Engine, NASA's APIs and
  cloud-hosted data) instead of downloaded in bulk. Polite crawlers fetch the
  PHIVOLCS bulletins and Tokyo's volcanic ash advisories.
- **Normalise:** a series per volcano for SO₂, heat, earthquakes and people
  living nearby, each labelled by instrument and shown with how much of the
  area the satellite could actually see.
- **Serve:** a map of the 24 active volcanoes, a page per volcano with every
  record on one time axis, and <LiveStoryCount url={frontmatter.dataVersionUrl} label="guided stories" />, among them Taal in January
  2020, in English and Japanese.
- **Integrity:** every chart is dated, PHIVOLCS alert levels appear only as
  dated markers "as reported by PHIVOLCS" with a link to the bulletin, and
  nothing describes a volcano today. When a series is empty, the volcano page
  says why.

## Architecture

Standard pattern: Jamstack delivery of materialised read models (the read side
of CQRS), with the pipeline as a batch layer, plus a read-only Spring Boot
service in the hexagonal (ports and adapters) style. Customisations: Earth
Engine does the satellite reductions where the imagery is stored, instead of
downloading it. Each data version is validated, then published to GitHub Pages
with a Hugging Face mirror as the fallback. The Java 17 API is compiled to a
native image with GraalVM so it wakes fast on a free server, behind an edge cache. Every source follows the
workspace archiving standard: a registry, fallbacks and a private archive.

## Where it stands

Plumes is live at plumes.krtmtthw.dev. The overview map shows satellite heat
detections and earthquakes, filtered by month. Two more layers are on by
default: the monthly mean SO₂ from Sentinel-5P, and ground motion along the
satellite's line of sight (COMET LiCSBAS), shown from the national zoom. Each of
the 24 volcanoes has a time axis from 2000, with a deep-time strip of its
recorded eruptions. There are <LiveStoryCount url={frontmatter.dataVersionUrl} label="guided stories" />, among them Taal in January
2020, and an About page with every source. PHIVOLCS alert levels come from its
public bulletins, one row a day. The read-only API sits behind an edge cache. The
source is [public on GitHub](https://github.com/abrycta/plumes).

Each data version holds the volcano records, stories, map layers and satellite
images. It goes live only when every check passes: each file against its schema,
one bulletin row a day with its source linked, and known facts such as PHIVOLCS's
alert level 4 for Taal on 12 January 2020. Behind them: 5,290 bulletin pages and 6,084 archive bulletins from
PHIVOLCS, 2,105 Tokyo ash advisories since 2010, and 649,248 NASA FIRMS heat
detections. Earth Engine use so far: about 10.0 compute-unit hours.

The Taal story, from the data: PHIVOLCS reported Alert Level 1 every day from
1 December 2019 to 11 January 2020, and raised it to level 4 in its 7:30 PM
bulletin on 12 January. The USGS catalogue went from no earthquake within 30 km
since October 2019 to 35 on 12 January. Tokyo's ash centre observed ash up to
about 16.8 km, and Sentinel-2 shows the crater lake drained.

Ash and cloud hide the satellites at the worst moments, so
coverage is shown beside every value. PHIVOLCS's online bulletins start in
September 2017 for Mayon, Kanlaon and Bulusan and in March 2019 for Taal, and
the newer ones (from July 2022) are only in its LAVA archive.
